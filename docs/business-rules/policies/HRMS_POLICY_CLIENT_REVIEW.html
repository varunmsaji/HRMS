02

Executive Summary

Attendance and Leave are both substantially built and internally consistent — but nine of the places where they make a judgment call are currently resolved by an engineering default, not a decision you've signed off on. One of those nine, whether overtime still counts on a day someone was also on approved leave, is significant enough to resolve before payroll ever depends on it.

What's implemented. Attendance calculates a daily status (Present / Half-Day / Absent / Leave / On-Duty / Incomplete) from punches, shift rules, holidays, weekly-offs, breaks and roster overrides, and separately detects overtime. Leave manages balances, accrual, carry-forward, multi-level approval, and comp-off, and hands its approved days to Attendance through one narrow, well-governed connection. Both modules resolve "whose rule applies to this employee" the same way — the most specific configured rule always wins — and both refuse to guess when required configuration is missing, rather than silently defaulting someone to Present or Working.

How this review is organised. Sections 5–6 walk through exactly how a day's attendance status and a leave application are calculated, step by step, with worked examples. Sections 7–8 catalogue every policy we found, tagged by whether it's fully working, partially working, or only stored and never read. Sections 9–11 show which rule wins whenever two policies could both apply, including the eleven-plus scenarios where Attendance and Leave directly meet. Sections 12–14 cover overtime, what happens when a policy changes after records already exist, and what "locked" actually means. Sections 15–17 are the parts most systems don't show a client at all: features that are half-built, places where old documentation and the real system disagree, and decisions the code is quietly making without anyone having agreed to them. Section 18 turns the highest-priority of those into decisions we're formally asking you to make, each with options, our recommendation, and space for your answer. Section 19 lists all 30 open decisions in one register; Section 20 is a single reference matrix of every precedence rule; Section 21 is where you sign off.

First pass

  1. Read the current behaviour sections (5–14) to understand what the system actually does today.
  2. Check the policy tables (7–8) for anything specific to your business you expected to see and don't.
  3. Review what overrides what (9–11) — this is where most "wait, really?" moments come from.

Second pass — decisions

  1. Read the open examples and gaps (15–17) so a decision's context isn't a surprise.
  2. Work through Section 18 decision by decision: approve our recommendation, choose a listed alternative, or describe what you want instead.
  3. Use Section 19 as the master checklist, and Section 21 to record formal sign-off.

04

System Overview

Both modules sit on the same foundation — a single "most specific rule wins" resolution engine used for every policy in the system — and meet at exactly one seam. Nothing below is aspirational; every box and arrow traces to a specific code path cited later in this document.

FoundationAn 8-level scope walk (Employee > Shift > Designation > Grade > Department > Branch > Legal Entity > Company) resolves which policy version applies to a given employee, on a given date, for a given policy type. Both Attendance and Leave call the identical engine.
Leave moduleOwns leave types, policies, the balance ledger, applications, and multi-level approval. On final approval, it writes one record — an approved day, half-day, or cancellation — into Attendance's own mirror table, inside the same database transaction as the approval itself.
Attendance moduleOwns the calendar. Combines punches, shift rules, holidays, weekly-offs, roster overrides and the leave day it received from Leave into one daily status, then separately detects overtime from the same punches.
OvertimeAttendance detects, classifies, caps and routes overtime for approval — entitlement only. No monetary amount is calculated anywhere in either module (Section 12).
Approval & lockRegularizations, overtime and leave each carry their own approval chain. Once a pay period is finalized and locked, it is read-only to every one of these paths — the single rule that outranks everything else in the system (Section 14).
PayrollOut of scope for this review and absent from the codebase today. Attendance and Leave both stop at paid-day fractions, LOP days, and OT entitlement minutes — the monetary conversion is a future module's job.

A full diagram of how the two modules' data actually connects is in Section 10 (Override Hierarchy) and Section 11 (Attendance–Leave Interaction).

05

How Attendance Works

Transcribed directly from the calculation engine's actual evaluation order (attendance/calculation.py::evaluate_day) — not an idealised process. Every step below runs, in this order, for every employee, every day.

We follow one employee, Ananya Rao, through a working example across all eight steps. Where a step depends on policy numbers your company configures (grace minutes, late bands, hour bands), we've used illustrative figures — labelled as such — to show how the mechanism works. The one number below that is an actual system default, not an illustration, is called out explicitly.

Step A‑01

Is the employee even in scope today?

What the system checks
Whether the date falls inside the employee's Employment.joining_date–last_working_day window. This reads the Employment record only — it deliberately does not look at whether the employee is on probation or confirmed.
Policy involved
Employment population gate (ATT-POL-052)
Can be overridden?
No — this is terminal. If the employee is NOT_JOINED or EXITED for the date, nothing below runs at all; there is no attendance day to calculate.
If information is missing
Not possible to reach this step without an Employment record exi­sting — a day is only ever evaluated for a date already inside a known employment window.
Example
Ananya joined on 2026-03-01 and has no exit date. For today's date, 2026-09-07, she is ACTIVE — the day proceeds to Step A-02.
Step A‑02

Is every setting needed to evaluate the day actually in place?

What the system checks
That a work calendar resolves for the employee's branch/company, and that every policy type (attendance, overtime, regularization) resolves via the 8-level scope walk. If either is missing, the day is marked CONFIG_ERROR and stops there.
Policy involved
Fail-closed configuration resolution (ATT-POL-054) — a confirmed design principle, not a bug: the system will not silently assume "Working" or "Present" when it doesn't actually know.
Can be overridden?
No — the only fix is HR completing the missing configuration (assigning a calendar or a policy at some scope that resolves for this employee).
What happens if missing
The day is left in CONFIG_ERROR, visible to HR as a data-quality problem, and cannot be finalized until resolved.
Example
Ananya's branch has a holiday calendar assigned, and an Attendance Policy resolves for her at the Department scope. Configuration is complete — the day proceeds.
Step A‑03

Has HR specifically scheduled this person differently today?

What the system checks
Whether a published roster row exists for this employee and date marking it as a week-off override. If so, the day is NOT_EXPECTED — and this wins even if the calendar says it's an ordinary working day.
Policy involved
Roster week-off override (ATT-POL-044)
Overrides
The calendar's default working-day classification (Step A-05) — a schedule fact HR entered by name beats the calendar's general pattern.
Can be overridden further?
Only by a full-day approved leave later in the sequence (Step A-07).
Example
No roster override exists for Ananya on 2026-09-07 — the day proceeds under the calendar's default classification.
Step A‑04

Does a shift resolve for this employee today?

What the system checks
Whether a ShiftVersion resolves from the employee's base assignment (or a roster override, for the day's actual worked schedule). If none resolves, the missing-shift policy decides between ABSENT and INCOMPLETE.
Policy involved
Missing-shift behaviour (ATT-POL-007)
What can override it
Punches on record for the day can still produce a presence-only read even without a resolved shift, depending on policy.
Example
Ananya is assigned a Fixed shift, 09:30–18:30. The shift resolves — the day proceeds to the calendar check.
Step A‑05

What does the calendar say — holiday, weekly off, or working day?

What the system checks
The date against the assigned holiday calendar and weekly-off pattern. A calendar exception (shutdown, special-working, substitute or cancelled holiday) is checked first and can override the base pattern.
Policy involved
Work calendar / day-type resolution (ATT-POL-038), Holiday types (ATT-POL-039), Weekly-off patterns (ATT-POL-042), Holiday-vs-weekly-off precedence (ATT-POL-043)
Precedence when holiday lands on a weekly off
The day stays classified as weekly-off, with a holiday_on_weekly_off flag raised for visibility. The holiday does not promote or substitute the day — there is no automatic day-in-lieu (confirmed design; see Decision 7 in Section 18 on whether a substitute day off should exist).
What can override this classification
A shift explicitly flagged to operate_on_holiday/operate_on_weekly_off, or a published roster override, both win over the calendar's default.
Example
2026-09-07 is an ordinary working Monday on Ananya's calendar — no holiday, no weekly-off. The day proceeds to punch evaluation.
Step A‑06

What do the punches say?

What the system checks
Raw punches are first paired (in/out), duplicate punches within a short window are collapsed, then worked minutes are run through the company's configured hour bands (Present / Half-Day / Absent by total minutes worked), late-arrival bands and early-departure bands (minutes late/early beyond a grace period), and, for flexible shifts, a core-hours breach check. None of these mutually suppress each other — any one can push the day to a worse status.
Policy involved
Grace-in/out minutes (ATT-POL-001) — the coded default is 0 minutes unless your company configures otherwise; Late/Early bands (ATT-POL-002/003); Hour bands (ATT-POL-004); Duplicate-punch collapse (ATT-POL-009, default 60 seconds); Core-hours breach (ATT-POL-015)
What happens if a punch is missing
Governed by missing_punch_behaviour — the coded default is INCOMPLETE, deliberately not ABSENT, because "a zero-punch day silently becoming Absent is too harsh to be an implicit default" (documented rationale). HR must explicitly opt into harsher handling.
Result
A baseline attendance_status, worked minutes, late minutes, early-departure minutes, and any break/core-hours flags.
Example configuration (illustrative, not a system default)
Suppose Ananya's shift starts 09:30 with a 10-minute grace-in, and her policy's late bands are: 0–15 min late after grace → flagged only; 16–60 min → Half-Day; >60 min → Absent. Ananya punches in at 09:47 — 7 minutes late after the 10-minute grace window. That falls in the 0–15 band: she is flagged late, but her baseline status from the hour band (she worked a full day) stays Present.
Step A‑07

Is there approved leave for this day?

What the system checks
Whether an active Leave Day Projection exists for the employee and date — a record Attendance owns but the Leave module writes, the moment a leave application clears its final approval stage.
Policy involved
Leave Day Projection overlay (ATT-POL-051), Work-on-leave behaviour (ATT-POL-013)
Overrides
Everything above it — including the calendar's NOT_EXPECTED classification from Step A-05 and the roster override from Step A-03. Under the default policy setting (KEEP_LEAVE), leave wins outright regardless of what punches say; late/early minutes are zeroed. Under the non-default PRESENT_CANCEL_LEAVE_HINT setting, the punch-derived status from Step A-06 is kept instead, and a flag is raised for Leave to see — but the leave application itself is never auto-cancelled by Attendance.
What it does not touch
The underlying calendar_day_type field is left exactly as Step A-05 set it, and overtime minutes detected in Step A-06 are not cleared — this is the single most important open decision in this review (Section 18, Decision 1).
Example
No leave is approved for Ananya on 2026-09-07, so this step makes no change — her day stays Present, with a late flag, from Step A-06.
Step A‑08

Is there an approved on-duty record?

What the system checks
An approved ON_DUTY regularization (e.g. a client visit or official travel day) for the date.
Overrides
The punch-derived status from Step A-06, but loses to leave if both an approved leave and an approved on-duty record somehow cover the same date.
Example
No on-duty record exists — no change. Ananya's final calculated status for the day is Present, flagged late, worked minutes and any detected overtime as computed in Step A-06.

Regularization and Overtime are not steps in this chain. A regularization is never a "correction" applied on top of the sequence above — an approved regularization adds new, immutable punch records and the entire sequence A-01–A-08 re-runs against the new punch set. Overtime is a separate, parallel calculation that runs independently and is never itself overridden by any of the eight steps above (see Section 12).

One rule sits outside this entire chain and outranks it: once the pay period covering this date is locked, none of Steps A-01–A-08 can execute at all for that date — not punches, not roster, not regularization, not leave-projection sync. See Section 14.

How Leave Works

Transcribed directly from LeaveApplicationService.submit() and _approve_internal() — the actual code order, not an idealised process. We follow a second employee, Rahul Mehta, applying for three days of Sick Leave, through submission and approval.

Step L‑01

Which policy applies, and does the calendar resolve?

What happens
The same 8-level scope walk used by Attendance resolves the LeavePolicyVersion for this employee, this leave type, this date. The work calendar is resolved for every date in the requested range.
Policy involved
Shared 8-level policy resolution (SHARED-POL-001)
If information is missing
Fails closed — submission is rejected outright if either the policy or the calendar doesn't resolve. No guessing.
Example
Rahul's Sick Leave policy resolves at the Department scope. The calendar resolves for his branch. Submission proceeds.
Step L‑02

Is the submitter allowed to submit this, for this person?

What happens
Checks whether the actual submitter (the employee themselves, their manager, or HR) is permitted to submit on this employee's behalf, per the resolved policy's on-behalf flags.
Policy involved
On-behalf submission permissions (LEAVE-POL-004)
Example
Rahul is submitting for himself — allow_employee_request is enabled. Proceeds.
Step L‑03

Is the employee on probation, and does this leave type allow that?

What happens
Checks Employee.status directly — if PROBATION and the resolved policy's allowed_during_probation is false, the application is blocked outright, regardless of who submitted it (self, manager-on-behalf, or HR-on-behalf).
Policy involved
Probation restriction on application (LEAVE-POL-002)
Overrides
Cannot be bypassed by an on-behalf submission — the check applies before that distinction matters. The only unblock path is EmployeeService.confirm_employee moving the employee to ACTIVE.
Worth noting
Leave reads Employee.status for this check; Attendance never does — the two modules use independent signals for "is this employee active" (Section 16).
Example
Rahul was confirmed to ACTIVE six months ago. Proceeds.
Step L‑04

Is the request itself well-formed?

What happens
In order: half-day validity (only permitted for a single-date application), reason/attachment required if the policy demands it, the backdating window (max_backdated_days, unlimited if unset), and the monthly request cap (excluding cancelled applications).
Policy involved
Half-day mechanics (LEAVE-POL-018), reason/attachment (LEAVE-POL-023), backdating window (LEAVE-POL-020), monthly cap (LEAVE-POL-022)
Notable gap
Backdating window only limits how far back a request can be submitted — it does not check whether the attendance period for that date is already locked. That's checked much later, only at approval (see Step L-08, and Decision 4 in Section 18).
Example
Rahul applies for three consecutive future dates, attaches no document (Sick Leave under 3 days doesn't require one under his company's policy), and this is his first request this month. All checks pass.
Step L‑05

Which requested dates actually consume balance?

What happens
Each date in the range is classified: WORKING/SPECIAL_WORKING days consume leave; WEEKLY_OFF/HOLIDAY/SHUTDOWN days do not — unless the sandwich rule applies (a non-consuming day bracketed on both sides by consuming leave days within the same application is pulled back into consumption).
Policy involved
Holiday/weekly-off interaction + sandwich rule (LEAVE-POL-019, sandwich_rule_enabled default true)
Scope limit
The sandwich look-back only ever considers dates within this one application — it never looks at a separately-submitted adjacent application (Decision area in Section 18).
If nothing consumes
Fails closed — a request whose entire range resolves to zero consuming days is rejected as an invalid date range.
Example
Rahul's three requested days are all ordinary working days — no holiday or weekly-off inside the range, so the sandwich rule is irrelevant here. All 3 days consume balance.
Step L‑06

Does this clash with another leave type already booked?

What happens
Checked only against an explicit LeaveCombinationRule row with allowed=False between this leave type and another type the employee already has active on an overlapping date. Absence of a rule means the combination is allowed.
Policy involved
Leave combination rule (LEAVE-POL-006)
Known gap
This only ever governs different leave types. Two overlapping applications of the same type are not blocked by any rule today — Decision 5, Section 18.
Example
Rahul has no other active leave application on these dates. Proceeds.
Step L‑07

Is there enough balance?

What happens
Balance is not a stored number — it's the live sum of every entry in the employee's append-only leave ledger. If reserve_balance_on_submission is enabled, other pending (SUBMITTED) applications of the same type are netted first.
Policy involved
Balance = live ledger sum (LEAVE-POL-007), Negative balance never allowed (LEAVE-POL-015)
Can be overridden?
No. There is no override path — the balance check is a hard block, unconditionally, for every submitter type.
Example
Rahul's Sick Leave ledger shows a running balance of 8.0 days. Requesting 3.0 leaves 5.0 remaining — sufficient. Proceeds to approval-chain resolution.
Step L‑08

Who approves, and in what order?

What happens
The configured chain (AUTO / MANAGER / MANAGER_THEN_HR / MANAGER_THEN_HOD_THEN_HR / HR) resolves real identities as of the submission date. Any stage whose identity can't be resolved (no manager on file, employee is their own department head) is silently dropped; if every stage is unresolved, the chain falls back to a single HR stage.
Policy involved
Multi-level approval chain (LEAVE-POL-027), resolution-failure fallback (LEAVE-POL-028), HOD self-head loop guard (LEAVE-POL-029)
Always-on rule
Self-approval is blocked unconditionally at every stage — no policy setting can re-enable it.
Example
Rahul's policy is MANAGER_THEN_HR. His manager is resolved — the application enters SUBMITTED, routed to his manager first.
Step L‑09

Approval: staged vs. final

What happens
A non-final stage approval only advances approval_stage — no ledger or attendance effect yet. Only the final stage's approval triggers a ledger debit (unless the leave type doesn't require balance) and syncs the approved day(s) into Attendance's Leave Day Projection, inside the same database transaction as the approval write.
Policy involved
Leave→Attendance projection sync (LEAVE-POL-033)
What can block final approval
If the attendance period covering any of the dates is already closed, the sync is refused and the entire approval transaction rolls back — the application stays SUBMITTED, never partially approved (Section 14).
Example
Rahul's manager approves — stage advances to HR, no ledger effect yet. HR then approves: this is the final stage. The attendance period for these dates is still open, so the ledger debits 3.0 days and the projection syncs into Attendance in one atomic step. Rahul's balance is now 5.0; Attendance will show those three dates as LEAVE the next time it calculates them.

Rejection is terminal at every stage. If HR rejects after the manager already approved, the rejection stands — there is no "send it back" step, and no ledger or attendance effect occurs.

Cancellation is the only reversal. There is no separate "withdraw" or "amend" action. The original submitter (only) can cancel a SUBMITTED application with no side effect, or an APPROVED one, which reverses the ledger debit and cancels the Attendance projection — but only while the attendance period is still open. If it's closed, cancellation is refused the same way approval is (Section 14).

Override Hierarchy

We went looking for a hierarchy shaped like Organization → Branch → Department → Grade/Designation → Employment Category → Shift/Roster → Employee → Approved Transaction → HR Manual Override → Locked Record. What we actually found is simpler and, in one respect, inverted: there is exactly one scope-resolution hierarchy, reused by every policy in both modules, and it runs from most specific to least specific — not top-down by organisational seniority. There is no separate "Employment Category" scope tier anywhere in the code; Grade and Designation are the closest equivalents.

The one confirmed hierarchy (SHARED-POL-001) — most specific wins

  1. Employee-specific assignment — a policy set for this one person by name
  2. Shift — the policy tied to the employee's assigned shift
  3. Designation
  4. Grade
  5. Department
  6. Branch
  7. Legal Entity
  8. Company (the fallback, least specific) every policy type resolves through this exact order

Sitting outside this ladder entirely, in ascending order of authority:

1. An approved transaction (a regularization or OT approval, a leave approval) is not a competing policy assignment — it's a one-time action that executes strictly within whatever the resolved policy already allows. It cannot approve an OT amount above the configured cap, or a regularization type outside the policy's allowed list (Section 9, CONF-031/032).

2. HR manual actions (approving, rejecting, publishing a new policy version, reopening a period) are the same kind of transaction-level action as above — not a distinct "override" layer with its own precedence. The one place HR has unilateral, unscoped authority is publishing a more specific policy assignment, which is just the ladder above working as designed.

3. A locked, historical record beats every single thing above it, including HR manual actions. This is the one rule with no exception found anywhere in either module — see Section 14.

Comp-off: Attendance emits, Leave decides

Attendance records approved OT convertible to time-off as a read-only fact (minutes + expiry date). Leave's own sweep converts it to balance using its configured day-length — and skips entirely, rather than guessing, if that isn't configured. Clean one-directional handoff, no conflict.

Re-syncing the same application is idempotent

If Leave re-syncs the same application ID (e.g. after a correction), the existing projection row updates in place — no duplicates, and flipping paid/unpaid correctly flips the paid/LOP split.

Overlapping projections are rejected

Cancelling reverts to the punch-derived truth

Overtime Policy

Confirmed architectural decision: Attendance determines and records overtime entitlement — duration, classification, approval, and the applicable policy/rate reference. Payroll is responsible for the final monetary overtime amount, using the employee's actual wage and statutory/company payroll rules. Attendance does not, and by design should not, calculate money. No Payroll module exists in this codebase today — the boundary is real, but the other side of it hasn't been built yet.

Eligibility

Governed by an eligibility mode: Never and Shift-flagged are both fully coded. Always and Grade-based are declared options that both simply fall through to "always eligible" — there is no code path that actually checks an employee's grade. If your company plans to use grade-based OT eligibility, this needs to be built, not just configured (Section 15).

Calculation

Worked minutes beyond the shift's scheduled end are the starting point. A configurable minimum-minutes threshold discards trivial excess; the remainder is rounded per policy, then clamped against daily, weekly and monthly caps. The weekly/monthly caps are computed by looking at sibling records for the period, which means recomputing one day's OT after the fact is not perfectly idempotent if neighbouring days have also changed — a documented, known characteristic, not a bug.

Classification

Each OT record is classified by day-class — Normal, Holiday, Weekly-Off, or Night — which determines which informational rate row applies for later payroll reference. Holiday and Weekly-Off classification work correctly, driven by the calendar. Night classification does not work as most people would expect it to: it is only ever derived from the employee's shift being flagged as crossing midnight. Overtime worked late at night on an ordinary day-shift is never classified as Night, no matter what time the punches actually show. The fields for a real clock-time night window exist in the schema and are configurable in the UI — they are simply never read (Section 15, Section 18 Decision 6).

Approval

Follows the same manager/HR approval pattern as regularization, with self-approval blocked unconditionally at every stage. A "requires pre-approval" flag exists on the policy but is never enforced by any code path — OT is detected and recorded after the fact regardless of this setting's value.

Caps & overrides

Daily/weekly/monthly caps clamp the detected amount before it ever reaches an approver — there is no per-request waiver path around a configured cap. A terminal OT record (approved, auto-approved, or rejected) is never revised by a later recompute, even if the OT policy changes afterward.

Comp-off — the boundary with Leave

When OT is approved as "time off instead of pay," Attendance emits the converted minutes and an expiry date as a read-only fact. Leave's own comp-off sweep is what actually converts that into leave balance days, using its own configured day-length — and, consistent with the fail-closed principle running through this whole audit, it skips the conversion entirely rather than guessing if that day-length isn't configured.

We deliberately do not describe a universal statutory multiplier (such as "2×") here — the informational rate rows in the system are configurable per company and were not independently verified against any specific jurisdiction's statutory requirement as part of this review.

Locking, Finalization & Corrections

The record lifecycle actually implemented is simpler than a typical Draft→Submitted→Approved→Processed→Finalized→Locked→Reopened→Adjusted chain. What exists is:

OPENAn attendance period's default state. Punches, regularizations, roster changes and leave-projection syncs all execute freely, subject only to their own individual policies.
VALIDATED / VALIDATION_FAILEDFinalize first runs a validation pass against six typed blocking conditions (e.g. unresolved INCOMPLETE days under a policy that requires them resolved). Any single blocker fails the whole attempt, atomically — nothing is left half-finalized.
CLOSEDThe period is locked. This is the one rule that outranks everything else in the entire system. No punch, no regularization (submission or approval), no OT approval, no roster change, and no leave approval or cancellation can touch a date inside a closed period — not for any reason, not for any role.
REOPENEDA deliberately separate, higher-permission action from closing, requiring a reason. Reopening only removes the lock — it does not restore anything or recompute anything automatically. Days become mutable again, but any day HR doesn't manually recompute keeps whatever numbers it had at the moment it was locked, even if a policy has since changed underneath it.

Can a locked day be changed?

No, by any of the mutation paths this audit checked, without first reopening the whole period. There is no single-record "fix just this one day" mechanism — the unit of unlock is the entire period.

Who can lock / unlock?

Two distinct, separately-grantable permissions. Locking requires passing validation; unlocking requires a stated reason. The separation is deliberate — reopening is treated as the riskier action.

Is there an audit trail?

Yes — every lock-lifecycle transition (Opened, Validated, Validation Failed, Closed, Reopened) is logged, as is every regularization/OT/leave approval action and every calendar-exception or roster-override edit.

Does reopening trigger recompute?

No, and this is the single biggest correction-workflow gap this review found. A reopened period can silently carry stale numbers indefinitely if nobody remembers to manually recompute — there is no system-enforced "reopen checklist." See Section 18, Decision 2.

Manual correction and regularization are the same feature. We looked for a distinct "manual correction" path separate from the regularization workflow, as the review framework for this document assumes exists. It doesn't — every correction to punches or status flows through regularization. An approved regularization doesn't edit anything; it adds new, immutable punch records and triggers a full recalculation of the day in the same transaction.

PD-XMOD-001 · Critical · Cross-module

Decision 1 — Leave and overtime, same day

Unresolved
The question

If an employee has approved leave for a day, and also has punches that would independently qualify for overtime that same day, should the overtime still be credited — in full, reduced, or not at all?

Why this needs your answer

This is the single most financially significant undecided rule in either module. Nobody has told the system what "worked overtime on a day you were also approved to be away" should mean, and it will happen in real operation — a half-day leave with unusually long hours on the worked half, a leave application approved after the employee already clocked in, or simple data-entry timing.

Current system behaviour

Both facts are recorded as true, completely independently. The leave overlay zeroes late-arrival and early-departure minutes when leave wins the day, but it does not touch detected overtime minutes. This was not a considered choice about overtime specifically — it's the absence of one.

Options
A — Leave always zeroes OT
Impact
No overtime is ever credited on a day marked Leave, regardless of policy setting.
Advantages
Simplest rule to explain; removes any incentive to pad hours on a leave day.
Risks
Penalises a legitimate case — e.g. someone who worked a long morning before an afternoon's approved leave.
B — Keep current (fully independent)
Impact
No change — OT and Leave remain two unrelated facts.
Advantages
Zero implementation cost.
Risks
Real payroll exposure the first time this combination reaches a payslip; no policy to point to if challenged.
C — Tie OT to the existing leave-overlay settingRecommended
Impact
Reuse the policy switch that already exists for this exact fork: under KEEP_LEAVE (the default), OT is zeroed along with late/early — consistent with leave already winning the whole day. Under PRESENT_CANCEL_LEAVE_HINT, OT is left as detected, consistent with punches already winning status in that mode.
Advantages
No new configuration surface; the rule follows a switch HR already understands and has already set; internally consistent with how late/early already behave.
Risks
Still requires an engineering change to read this flag in the OT-detection pass; a company using the non-default setting needs to understand OT now follows it too.
Recommendation
RecommendedOption C — govern OT with the existing work-on-leave setting

This is the option most consistent with a rule you've already configured, cheapest to explain to employees ("if leave already wins your day, it wins your overtime too"), and avoids inventing a second, competing setting for what is really the same underlying question the system already asks. It also keeps the door open for a future per-leave-type override if you later want, say, emergency medical leave to behave differently from planned annual leave.

PD-LOCK-2 · High · Attendance

Decision 2 — Fixing a mistake after payroll closes

Unresolved
The question

Today, the only way to correct any attendance record after its pay period is locked is to reopen the entire period. Should a narrower, single-record correction mechanism exist instead?

Why this needs your answer

A full reopen has a large blast radius — it makes every record in the period mutable again, not just the one that needs fixing — and, per Section 14, does not auto-recompute anything. A day nobody remembers to manually recompute can silently carry stale numbers indefinitely. The bigger the payroll cycle, the more this risk compounds.

Current system behaviour

No per-record correction path exists. The only remedy is: reopen the whole period (a separate, higher-permission action requiring a stated reason) → manually identify and recompute every affected day → re-lock.

Options
A — Keep current (full reopen only)
Impact
No change.
Advantages
No engineering cost; a blunt but simple rule.
Risks
Growing operational risk as payroll cycle size and correction frequency increase; the stale-recompute gap remains open indefinitely.
B — Build a scoped correction/arrears recordRecommended
Impact
A new, auditable object that records a correction against one locked day without unlocking the period around it — likely flowing into the next open payroll cycle rather than reopening the closed one.
Advantages
Minimises blast radius; keeps the audit trail explicit; removes the stale-recompute risk entirely for this use case.
Risks
Real engineering investment; needs to be designed jointly with the eventual Payroll module, since "which cycle absorbs the correction" is a payroll question as much as an attendance one.
C — Time-boxed reopen window
Impact
Periods remain reopenable only within N days of locking; after that, even a full reopen is disabled.
Advantages
Cheaper than Option B; caps how long a period stays exposed to correction.
Risks
Doesn't fix the underlying blast-radius or stale-recompute problem — just narrows the window it can occur in.
Recommendation
RecommendedOption B — a scoped correction mechanism, once Payroll scope is defined

Option B is the only one that actually removes the risk rather than bounding it, and this system's whole design philosophy already favours narrow, auditable, reversible actions over blunt ones (Section 10). We'd sequence this behind your Payroll module design, since the two need to agree on which cycle a late correction lands in.

PD-XMOD-004 · High · Cross-module · coupled to Decision 2

Decision 3 — Leave's own remedy when the lock blocks an approval

Unresolved
The question

Should Leave get an equivalent narrow path for an application that couldn't sync because its date's attendance period was already locked — short of a full attendance-period reopen?

Why this needs your answer

Today, an employee whose leave approval hits a locked period is stuck: the application sits SUBMITTED forever unless someone reopens the whole attendance period on their behalf (Section 11, AL-010).

Current system behaviour

None — the only remedy is the same full Attendance reopen described in Decision 2.

Options
A — None (current)
Impact
No change.
Risks
Leave inherits the exact same blast-radius problem as Decision 2, with no leave-specific remedy at all.
B — Extend Decision 2's mechanism to LeaveRecommended, conditional
Impact
Once a scoped correction/arrears object exists on the Attendance side (Decision 2, Option B), Leave's sync path targets that object instead of requiring the whole period open.
Advantages
One mechanism serves both modules; no duplicate design effort.
Risks
Blocked on Decision 2's outcome — this can't be built independently.
Recommendation
RecommendedOption B, sequenced directly behind Decision 2

This is architecturally downstream of Decision 2 — we'd recommend deciding them together rather than in isolation.

PD-XMOD-002 · High · Cross-module

Decision 4 — Temporary shift changes and which policy applies

Unresolved
The question

When HR temporarily reassigns someone to a different shift for a single day (a roster override), should that shift's own specific policy settings (grace minutes, late bands, etc.) apply for that day — instead of the settings tied to the employee's usual shift?

Why this needs your answer

This is a documented conflict, not just a gap: the original architecture explicitly says policy resolution should follow the roster shift, and the code doesn't do that. Someone reading only the architecture docs would reasonably expect different behaviour than what actually happens.

Current system behaviour

The employee works the roster shift's schedule (roster wins for the actual hours), but any grace/band/missing-punch policy scoped specifically to that roster shift is never picked up — policy resolution still uses their permanent, base-assignment shift.

Options
A — Keep current (base assignment only)
Advantages
No change needed.
Risks
Leaves the documented conflict open indefinitely; an employee working a stricter shift's hours may still be judged by their usual, more lenient shift's grace period, or vice versa.
B — Align policy resolution to the roster shiftRecommended
Impact
SHIFT-scoped policy resolution checks the day's roster override first, falling back to the base assignment only if no roster override exists.
Advantages
Matches the original architecture's stated intent; matches what an HR admin would reasonably assume already happens; the actual hours worked and the actual policy applied become consistent.
Risks
A real code change to the resolution engine, requiring re-testing of every SHIFT-scoped policy path; could change already-calculated (but unlocked) days' numbers the moment it ships.
C — Make it configurable
Impact
A company-level toggle chooses base-assignment vs. roster for policy resolution.
Risks
Adds a second, harder-to-explain configuration surface for what should probably just be correct by default.
Recommendation
RecommendedOption B — align to the roster shift

The architecture already promised this; closing the gap removes a standing documentation/implementation conflict rather than papering over it with a new setting.

PD-XMOD-003 · High · Cross-module

Decision 5 — Backdated leave for an already-closed pay period

Unresolved
The question

Should a leave application be blocked immediately at submission if its target date's attendance period is already locked — instead of accepting the submission and only failing much later, at final approval?

Why this needs your answer

The current ordering is confusing in practice: an employee and their manager can go through an entire approval chain, believing everything is fine, only to have the final HR approval fail with no earlier warning.

Current system behaviour

The backdating-window check only limits how far back a submission can be dated — it never checks whether that date's attendance period is locked. The lock is only discovered when the sync is attempted at final approval.

Options
A — Keep current (fails late)
Risks
Wasted approval effort; a confusing failure for whoever is doing the final approval, with no context for why.
B — Block at submissionRecommended
Impact
Submission itself checks the target date's attendance-period status and refuses immediately if closed, with a clear message.
Advantages
Consistent with the fail-closed principle already used everywhere else in both modules (Sections 5 & 6); saves everyone's time.
Risks
Minor engineering cost; needs a clear error message so employees understand why a legitimate-seeming request was refused.
C — Warn, don't block
Impact
Submission proceeds with a visible warning; the hard failure still only happens at approval.
Risks
Doesn't actually prevent the wasted-effort problem, just labels it earlier.
Recommendation
RecommendedOption B — fail closed at submission

Matches the system's own established pattern of refusing to accept a request it already knows can't succeed, rather than deferring the bad news.

PD-ATT-001 · High · Attendance, technical

Decision 6 — Two policy assignments accidentally overlapping

Unresolved
The question

If two active policy assignments ever exist at the exact same scope (e.g. two "Department: Engineering" attendance-policy assignments both active at once), should the system structurally prevent that from ever happening, or just document a tie-break rule for when it does?

Why this needs your answer

This shouldn't happen through the normal UI, but nothing in the database currently stops it — a race condition, a bulk-import script, or a manual data fix could create it. If it does happen, which one resolves is currently whatever order the database query happens to return, which is not a guarantee, not a rule.

Current system behaviour

No constraint, no documented tie-break. Undefined behaviour if it occurs.

Options
A — Add a database constraintRecommended
Impact
Make the overlap structurally impossible, the same way overlapping effective-dated versions are already prevented elsewhere in the system.
Advantages
Removes an entire class of "which one silently won" bugs at the source; consistent with how every other effective-dated table in this system already works.
Risks
Requires a migration; needs to handle any pre-existing bad data before the constraint can be added.
B — Document a tie-break rule only
Impact
e.g. "most recently created wins" — written down, not enforced.
Risks
Cheaper, but leaves the underlying data-integrity hole open.
C — Leave as-is
Risks
Not recommended — this is the only option that leaves genuinely undefined behaviour in production.
Recommendation
RecommendedOption A — structural prevention

Every other effective-dated object in this system already gets this protection; there's no principled reason for policy assignments to be the exception.

PD-ATT-002 · High · Attendance

Decision 7 — Night-shift overtime, by shift type or by the clock?

Unresolved
The question

Should overtime worked during genuine night hours get Night classification based on the actual time on the clock, regardless of shift type — instead of only when the whole shift is designated as crossing midnight?

Why this needs your answer

Earlier internal documentation called this "the sharpest contradiction between the system's original design intent and what was actually built." The configuration fields for a real night-time window exist and are editable today — they simply have no effect, which risks HR believing a setting is working when it isn't.

Current system behaviour

Night classification is derived exclusively from whether the employee's shift is flagged as crossing midnight. An ordinary day-shift employee who works genuinely late into the night on overtime is never classified as Night, no matter the actual clock time.

Options
A — Implement the real clock-time checkRecommended
Impact
Compare actual punch time against the configured night-window start/end, independent of the shift's cross-midnight flag.
Advantages
Makes the existing configuration fields actually do what they claim to do; correctly classifies genuine night work for any shift type.
Risks
Real engineering work in the OT classification pass; needs careful testing around shift boundaries and cross-midnight edge cases.
B — Remove the unused fields
Impact
Stop implying a capability that doesn't exist — hide or delete the night-window configuration.
Risks
Only appropriate if your company genuinely never needs clock-time-based night classification.
C — Keep as-is
Risks
Leaves a "false customization" surface live — HR can configure something that quietly does nothing.
Recommendation
RecommendedOption A, unless you confirm shift-flag-only is genuinely sufficient (then B)

If any part of your workforce works occasional or unscheduled night overtime outside a dedicated night shift, only Option A actually classifies it correctly. If night OT genuinely only ever happens on dedicated night shifts in your operation, Option B is the honest fallback — but Option C, leaving a non-functional setting configurable, is not recommended under any circumstance.

PD-LEAVE-001 · High · Leave

Decision 8 — Two requests, same leave type, overlapping dates

Unresolved
The question

Should two overlapping applications of the same leave type be blocked, the way overlapping applications of two different types already are when an explicit rule exists between them?

Why this needs your answer

Nothing today stops an employee submitting two Sick Leave requests for overlapping dates. Both can be independently approved, and both will independently debit the balance ledger — a duplicate-consumption risk that's easy to miss until a balance looks wrong.

Current system behaviour

The combination-rule engine only ever governs overlap between two different leave types with an explicit deny rule configured between them. Same-type overlap has no check at all.

Options
A — No block (current)
Risks
Duplicate balance consumption; confusing for HR reconciling a ledger against actual days taken.
B — Block same-type overlap by defaultRecommended
Impact
A second application for the same leave type on an already-covered date is rejected at submission, mirroring the existing cross-type rule.
Advantages
Closes an obvious double-booking hole; reuses logic and language the system already has for cross-type combination rules — low implementation cost.
Risks
Needs a sensible error message so an employee genuinely trying to extend a leave (rather than accidentally duplicating it) isn't confused — see the "no amend" gap noted in Section 15.
C — Configurable per leave type
Impact
Some leave types allow overlap (e.g. intermittent medical leave), others don't.
Risks
More flexible, but a new configuration surface for a case that's arguably always a mistake.
Recommendation
RecommendedOption B — block by default, consistent with the cross-type rule

It's hard to construct a legitimate business reason for two overlapping requests of the identical leave type; the risk of accidental duplicate balance consumption outweighs the flexibility Option C would add.
